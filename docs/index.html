Understanding and Mitigating Drone-Induced Distraction for Construction Safety

A research project quantifying how UAV flight behavior changes construction workers' visual attention and task performance, then translating the evidence into distraction-aware drone path planning.

Research Overview View Studies

Research overview

Reducing cognitive distraction from drones in safety-critical construction work

Drones are increasingly used for construction inspection, monitoring, mapping, and progress documentation. Their safety risk is usually framed as a direct physical hazard, such as collision or falling components. This project focuses on a less visible risk: drones can become visual, auditory, and cognitive distractors while workers perform tasks where attention to hazards is essential.

Human factors studied

  • Visual attention on hazards, sky, drone, and payload
  • Task performance, including collisions and completion time
  • Worker response under high-risk construction tasks

Flight parameters

  • Vertical height and relative position
  • Front/behind and above/below flight directions
  • Path choices around workers, tower cranes, and obstacles

Research gaps

What was missing

  • Prior construction studies focused mainly on close drone distances and psychological response.
  • Task-performance effects at normal UAV working heights were not well quantified.
  • Drone-induced distraction had not been represented as a factor in construction UAV path planning.

Objectives

What this project contributes

  1. Measure how drone flight height and direction affect worker hazard awareness.
  2. Quantify how drone position affects tower crane operator attention and collisions.
  3. Convert distraction evidence into soft costs for UAV path planning.

Research workflow

From behavioral evidence to planning

The project connects human-subject experiments with computational planning so that drone operation can be evaluated not only by physical feasibility, but also by its likelihood of distracting nearby workers.

Research motivation

Construction UAVs create a human-factors problem that is not captured by physical collision risk alone.

Distraction is closely tied to construction safety outcomes, especially falls, slips, trips, and crane-operation incidents. The project asks whether UAV flight choices can be designed so that drones still support inspection and monitoring tasks while reducing their interference with worker attention.

Empirical studies

Controlled experiments on drone-induced distraction in high-risk construction tasks

Two within-subject VR experiments were designed to isolate drone flight effects while controlling individual differences. Eye-tracking, task completion, collisions, and simulator performance metrics were used to connect visual attention with safety-relevant behavior.

Study 1

Workers at height

Participants performed a material-handling task in a virtual elevated construction environment containing openings and edges. The drone appeared at multiple working heights and from different directions, creating a controlled setting for testing whether UAV presence redirects attention away from hazard areas.

  • Within-subject design with Latin square ordering
  • 24 participants in a virtual construction site
  • Meta Quest Pro eye tracking and KAT Walk C2+ treadmill
Task Material handling near openings and edges
Conditions No drone, front/behind drone, 16/48/82 ft heights
Measures Fixation time/count on danger areas and sky

Study 1 video: Work-at-height experiment

Video documentation of the virtual elevated task environment and participant movement setup used to study hazard-area attention under drone flight conditions.

Demo focus: Participants move through a work-at-height environment where openings, edges, sky regions, and drone flight paths define areas of interest for eye-tracking analysis.

Why this setup matters

The VR environment makes it possible to expose every participant to the same hazardous task, drone height, and drone direction conditions without creating field risk. The within-subject design improves statistical power by comparing each participant against their own no-drone baseline.

Study 1 conclusion

Low, front-facing drone flights create the clearest distraction signal for workers at height.

The results indicate that drone presence changes where workers look while navigating an elevated environment. The key safety implication is not only that workers notice drones, but that attention is redistributed away from danger areas that normally require continuous monitoring.

  • Use greater vertical clearance when flying near elevated work areas.
  • Avoid front-approach paths when workers are moving near openings or edges.
  • Represent worker distraction zones as spatial planning parameters rather than treating distraction as a purely subjective response.

Study 2

Tower crane operators

Participants performed an official certification-style zigzag crane task while drones flew inward and outward from above or below the control cabin. This study tested whether drone position affects operator visual attention and task performance in a precision-control activity.

  • Within-subject design with five flight conditions
  • 20 participants in a tower crane simulator
  • Performance measured by task time, collisions, and payload deviation
Task Certification-style zigzag crane operation
Conditions No drone, above/below cabin, close/far horizontal distance
Measures Drone/payload fixation, collisions, completion time, payload deviation

Study 2 video: Tower crane operator experiment

Video documentation of the crane simulator task used to evaluate drone effects on operator visual attention, payload control, and boundary collisions.

Demo focus: The simulation recreates a precision-control crane task while drones approach from above or below the operator cabin, linking flight condition with operator attention and task performance.

Operational interpretation

For tower crane operation, drone height relative to the cabin becomes an operational design variable. Flights below the operator can interfere with payload-control performance even when the drone does not physically contact the crane.

Design implication

Drone flight height is a critical distraction parameter for crane tasks. Keeping drones vertically clear from active operator viewpoints and avoiding below-cabin paths can reduce distraction-related risk while still allowing site monitoring and inspection.

Study 2 conclusion

Tower crane operation is sensitive to cabin-relative drone height.

The crane task results connect drone position to both visual attention and operational performance. Below-operator flights are especially important because they can interfere with payload-control behavior without requiring any physical collision between drone and crane.

  • Avoid below-cabin drone paths when tower cranes are actively operated.
  • Separate UAV routing rules for crane work from generic site-level obstacle avoidance.
  • Use operator-relative flight zones when translating behavioral results into planning constraints.

Study 3 / Computational method

Distraction-aware drone path planning

The planning method converts empirical distraction parameters into soft costs in a 3D grid. The drone can still reach inspection or monitoring viewpoints, but routes that dwell in distraction areas become more expensive, allowing the planner to trade small efficiency losses for meaningful reduction in distraction exposure.

Study 3 video: Distraction-aware path planning

Video documentation of cost-aware UAV routing around workers, tower cranes, obstacles, travel points, and empirically derived distraction boundaries.

Demo focus: Behavioral findings from Study 1 and Study 2 become route costs, reducing drone dwell time near safety-critical work while preserving feasible inspection paths.

Cross-study synthesis

Empirical findings become planning parameters

The worker-at-height study and tower-crane study identify vertical clearance thresholds that can be represented spatially. These parameters define worker and crane distraction areas used by the path planner.

  • Worker distraction area: maintain substantial vertical clearance over workers performing high-risk tasks.
  • Tower crane distraction area: avoid below-operator flight paths near active crane operation.
  • Planning use: penalize routes that dwell inside distraction areas instead of treating them as rigid no-fly zones.

Method logic

Why use soft costs?

Construction sites often require drones to reach specific viewpoints for monitoring or inspection. A strict no-fly boundary may make a task infeasible or overly conservative. Soft costs preserve route feasibility while making distraction-heavy routes less attractive to the planner.

  • Hard constraints still represent obstacles and physical infeasibility.
  • Soft costs represent human-factors risk from drone-induced distraction.
  • The soft-cost weight controls the safety-efficiency tradeoff.

Planning tradeoff across site complexity

When the site includes more workers and tower cranes, the planner has more opportunities to reroute around distraction areas. Study 3 conclusion

Distraction-aware planning turns behavioral evidence into operational UAV guidance.

The computational study shows that drone paths can be optimized beyond physical feasibility. By adding empirically derived soft costs, the planner can reduce exposure near distraction-sensitive work zones while preserving route feasibility for construction monitoring tasks.

  • Soft costs provide a practical alternative to overly rigid no-fly boundaries.
  • Moderate detours can substantially reduce drone dwell time in distraction areas.
  • The method creates a direct bridge from human-subject evidence to UAV route planning.

Research contributions

Connecting behavioral evidence with safer UAV operation

The project contributes at three levels: understanding how drone presence affects worker attention, parameterizing distraction risk in spatial terms, and demonstrating how those parameters can change UAV route choices.

Understanding impacts

Shows how drone presence can shift visual attention away from hazards and alter task performance in high-risk construction settings, including work-at-height tasks and tower crane operation.

Quantitative parameters

Extracts spatial distraction parameters from controlled behavioral experiments, including worker vertical clearance and tower crane cabin-relative flight conditions.

Mitigation by planning

Integrates distraction costs into path planning to reduce drone dwell time in risky distraction areas while preserving route feasibility for inspection and monitoring tasks.

Future directions

Extending distraction-aware UAV safety research

Task diversity

Extend beyond work-at-height, roofing, and tower crane operation to tasks such as ladder work, scaffold inspection, equipment spotting, and collaborative lifting.

Drone behavior space

Study flight speed, lateral offset, trajectory shape, hovering duration, acoustic profile, lighting, and drone size as additional distraction factors.

Field validation

Test whether VR-derived distraction parameters transfer to live construction jobsites and can be integrated with real site sensing, inspection routes, and UAV operation policies.
